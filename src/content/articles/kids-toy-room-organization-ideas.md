A toy room only stays organized if the system works for the kid using it, not just for whoever set it up. That means fewer "correct" places for every item and more big, forgiving categories a young kid can actually manage without adult help every time.

## Bins beat folding and stacking, every time

The realistic bar for a young kid cleaning up isn't sorting neatly — it's getting things off the floor. Open bins support that: toys go in, done. Systems that require folding, stacking, or precise placement mostly end up not being used at all.

## Forward-facing books get picked, spine-out books don't

A young kid who can't read yet chooses books by cover, not by title on a spine. A forward-facing display means they can actually browse and pick independently, which matters more for building a reading habit than a tidy spine-out shelf does.

## Small pieces need their own containment strategy

LEGO and building blocks are the single most common "stepped on in the dark" toy category for a reason — loose pieces escape any general toy bin almost immediately. A dedicated case with actual compartments keeps them contained specifically.

## Stuffed animals need vertical space, not floor space

Stuffed animals pile up because they don't fit neatly in a standard bin and there's rarely a dedicated spot for them. Wall-mounted, they use space that's otherwise sitting empty instead of competing with everything else for floor or shelf room.

## Art supplies work best mobile, not fixed

A fixed art supply shelf means art only happens at that specific spot. A rolling cart goes wherever the activity is — the kitchen table, the floor, outside — and returns to its storage spot afterward instead of leaving supplies scattered wherever they were last used.
