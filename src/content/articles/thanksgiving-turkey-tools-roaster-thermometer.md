Most of what decides a Thanksgiving turkey is the recipe and the timing. The rest is a handful of tools that most kitchens only use once a year, and it's usually one of them that's missing on the day: no rack, no thermometer, nothing to get the fat off the gravy.

## A roasting pan with a rack

A rack lifts the bird off the bottom of the pan so hot air reaches the underside and it doesn't sit in its own juices. Stainless steel is the most useful choice, because you can put the pan straight on the stovetop afterwards and make the gravy from the browned drippings. Nonstick is easier to wash but browns those drippings less.

## Two thermometers, two jobs

The USDA's safe minimum for poultry is 165°F, checked with a food thermometer in the thickest part of the breast, the innermost part of the thigh and the innermost part of the wing. A pop-up timer or the clock can't tell you that. A leave-in probe tracks the temperature as the bird roasts, so you know when you're getting close. An instant-read thermometer then checks the other spots before it comes out.

## Gravy without the grease

A fat separator is the simplest gravy upgrade there is. Fat floats, and the separator pours from the bottom, so you keep the flavorful juices and leave the grease behind. A baster with an angled tip does the same kind of job during roasting, without tipping a heavy pan.

## Where it all goes afterwards

A roasting pan is often the largest pan in the kitchen and gets used once or twice a year. Store the rack inside it, and stand the pan upright in a cabinet with a bakeware divider rather than stacking it flat under everything else. ## Worth being direct about

None of these tools replaces a good recipe and enough time. A turkey takes hours, and thawing a frozen one takes days in the fridge, so plan backwards from when you want to eat. Measure your oven before buying a roaster: a 16-inch pan doesn't fit every oven, especially with a large bird in it.
