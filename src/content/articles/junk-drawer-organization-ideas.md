Every junk drawer fails the same way: it gets one bin, or no bins, meant to hold categorically unrelated things — batteries, tape, takeout menus, a phone charger nobody uses anymore. The fix isn't a single product; it's matching each real category of stuff to a compartment sized for it, which usually means combining two or three different organizers instead of buying one.

## Liner first, tray second

A tray dropped into a bare drawer still slides around every time the drawer opens, which is the actual mechanism behind a junk drawer slowly re-mixing itself over a few months. A thin non-slip liner underneath stops that movement for good, and it's the cheapest single item that makes everything else on this list actually stay where you put it.

## Long items need slots, not bins

Pens, scissors, a ruler, a mini screwdriver — anything longer than it is wide gets lost inside a square bin but sits visibly in a slotted tray. An expandable tray with long/medium/short slots is built specifically for this category and adjusts to fit whatever width drawer you actually have.

## Loose small stuff needs its own separate system

Batteries, twist ties, and spare hardware don't fit a slotted tray at all — they need small, individual bins that can be arranged and rearranged as what you're storing changes. This is a genuinely different organizing problem from the slotted-tray one above, which is why one product rarely solves a whole junk drawer by itself.

## Bulkier items get the leftover space

Whatever's left after the slots and small bins — tape rolls, a notepad, a stack of coupons — needs wider, fixed compartments rather than either of the above. Fitting the compartment to the item, instead of cramming everything into whatever tray you bought first, is the actual difference between a junk drawer that stays organized and one that's tidy for a week.
