Paper piles up because every sheet needs a small decision and there's nowhere obvious to put it while you make one. You don't need a complicated filing system. Every piece of paper in a house is one of three things: something to act on, something to keep, or something to destroy. Give each of those one home.

## Act on it: a spot for what just came in

Mail and forms need somewhere to land that isn't the kitchen counter. An upright sorter keeps them visible, and a section for each person or for "to pay" and "to file" is plenty. Stacked trays do the same job if you'd rather keep papers flat. Empty it once a week. For the rest of the desk, see [home office organization](/articles/home-office-organization-ideas/).

## Keep it: hanging files for this year

Hanging folders work because you can see every tab at once and add a folder without reshuffling the rest. Start with broad categories, such as taxes, home, car, medical and school, and split one only when it gets crowded. No filing cabinet? A file box with rails holds the same folders. If you only have a few papers, an accordion file may be all you need.

Keep one folder just for this year's tax paperwork and drop forms in as they arrive, so you aren't hunting for them later.

## Keep it, but not nearby: boxes for old years

At the end of each year, move what you're done with into a labeled archive box and put it on a closet shelf. Your active files stay thin, and old records are still there if you need them. How long to keep them depends on your situation: the [IRS guide to keeping records](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-long-should-i-keep-records) is the place to start, and a tax professional can answer specifics.

## Destroy it: shred, then recycle

Anything with an account number or personal details that you no longer need should be shredded, not just thrown away. A cross-cut shredder near the sorter makes this a one-step habit. For more on protecting that information, see [digital privacy and security essentials](/articles/digital-privacy-security-essentials/).

## The originals you can't replace

Passports, birth certificates, and similar originals don't belong in the general files. Keep them together in one bag or box that someone in the house could grab quickly, and make copies or scans in case the originals are lost.

## Worth being direct about

Fire ratings on document bags are the makers' claims. A bag can help, but it isn't a guarantee, so keep copies of your most important papers somewhere else too. This article isn't tax or legal advice. And no organizer sorts paper by itself: the system only works if you empty the sorter regularly, so choose the simplest setup you'll actually keep up with.
